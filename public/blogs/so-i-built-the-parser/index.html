So I built the parser

I promised a boring, perfect parser. So I built Statement2CSV: offline PDFs in, Money Manager CSV out, with local AI only writing labels.

Last post ended with a promise: my CSV importer is going to be a parser. Boring. Perfect. So I built it.

And yes, before anyone says it, I vibe-coded it. The guy who wrote “AI slop ruined software engineering” used AI to build his parser. I know. Sit down, let me explain, because the distinction is the entire point.

Also, to be clear: this is not a product. It is not a SaaS, not a launch, not a tool I am shipping to anyone. It is a scrappy little page I made for myself, because I had seven months of missing expenses and no patience. I call it Statement2CSV. I drop in my statements, review the transactions in a table, export a file, import it into Money Manager. That is the whole thing.

The first decision was the only one that really mattered: everything runs offline. The PDF never leaves my browser. These are my bank statements, the most sensitive files I own, and I was not about to upload them to someone’s server so a model could read my rent payments. PDF.js decrypts and parses everything locally. That one constraint shaped the whole build.

The parsing itself is pure 2010. No model touches the numbers. My three banks all lay out their statements differently, so the parser detects which bank a statement came from and applies the right rules for that format. Dates, descriptions, debits, credits, balances. Rules and regex. The stuff I almost skipped.

Then the export. I needed it to write exactly what Money Manager expects for its Excel backup import: Date, Account, Category, Subcategory, Note, Amount, Income/Expense, Description, with dates as MM/DD/YYYY. In the app it is More, then Backup, then Import Excel File, pick the file, done. I also made it spit out a generic CSV with debit/credit/balance columns, because future me will probably want this data in a spreadsheet too.

Now the part you are waiting for: yes, there is AI in it. Two small local models, Qwen3 4B and Llama, running through Ollama on my own machine. But they never touch the parsing. They only write better remarks. Bank descriptions look like “UPI/412983746501/P2A/AMZN” and I will not remember what that was three months from now, so the models turn them into human-readable labels. Keyword rules stay as the fallback, and anything the model is not confident about gets flagged amber for me to review by hand. The AI is a label maker. The parser does the work.

And that is the distinction I was trying to make in the last post. Vibe-coding some UI scaffolding for myself and letting a local model rewrite “UPI/P2M/...” into “groceries” is not slop. Slop is when you do not understand the problem and you rent a supercomputer to have the understanding for you. I understood the problem because I lived it, from the May phone wipe to the locked PDF to the local model that could not read it. The parser is mine. The AI just did the chores.

Seven months of transactions, three bank accounts, one clean import file. My expense history is back. And it only took remembering that sometimes the right tool is a few hundred lines of rules.

Boring. Perfect. Like I said.
